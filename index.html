Evaluation Pass Map

Only the $50K trades were backtested. Every size here is that same three years of trades scaled to the contracts the size allows. Four TradingView runs (4, 12, 16 and 24 micros, each with the daily limit and safety buffer scaled to match) reproduced the scaling exactly, which confirms the arithmetic rather than the trading; sizes outside 4 to 24 micros were not run and say so on the card. The size is chosen to keep the same risk as the $50K build: against the firm's daily limit where it publishes one, otherwise against its drawdown allowance. Where a firm publishes no daily limit above $50K, the $50K limit is scaled pro rata and the card says so.

What changed in this map, and when

Reading this map

  • 33% is the target. Green cells mean an evaluation started that month passed at least a third of the time. Amber and brown months are the ones to avoid buying into.
  • Attempts overlap. A new attempt starts every trading day and each runs 30 days, so a strong or weak stretch is counted many times. Treat a single cell as a hint, not a measurement.
  • The rules decide the outcome, not the brand. At $50K, plans with no consistency rule and a daily limit above about $1,000 sit near 30%; the same class sits near 25% at $100K and 24% at $150K, because the profit target rises faster than the drawdown allowance. A 40–50% consistency rule roughly halves that. A tight daily limit does not end a plan by itself: it forces a smaller position, and the card shows the size each plan is traded at.
  • Consistency chips say when the rule bites. An amber chip is a rule inside the challenge, and it roughly halves the pass rate. A teal chip is a rule that only starts once the account is funded, so it does not affect these numbers - but it shapes how you take payouts later.
  • Grey chips mark a rule that is not confirmed on the firm's own page - read from a third party, assumed pro rata by us, or a position size outside the range that was actually run. Confirm those on the firm's own dashboard before paying for an account.
  • The last twelve complete months are weaker than the three-year figure on most plans, by a median of about 3 points. Each card prints its own figure for that window with its 95% interval; the headline and the tables are the three-year numbers.
  • Every figure here assumes the backtest's fill model: 2 ticks of slippage, about half a point on MNQ. Nobody has yet measured what a real broker fills at. Re-running the whole history at worse fills gives a profit factor of 1.06 at 8 ticks per side and 0.96 - a loss - at 12, against 1.25 at 2. Break-even is near 10 ticks, and roughly two thirds of trades exit at the stop, so fill quality is the whole margin.
  • 8.5% of the trades sit on data that cannot be reproduced. These results are measured on MNQ1!, a continuous contract that switches to a new futures contract four times a year. Before each switch the old contract is thin; after it, the stored history for those dates carries the new contract's prices instead. about 79 trades - 8.5% of the record - fall in that window and they carry 24% of the profit, at a profit factor of 1.855 against 1.212 everywhere else. How much worse those fills really are is not known; at 9 points worse - an illustration, not a measurement - it would cost about $11,400 and take the profit factor to 1.206. Trade the dated contract (MNQZ6 and so on) rather than MNQ1! if you can.
  • This is backtest history, not a forecast. The whole window is the strategy's own tuning period, so the forward test is what settles it.
  • Payout figures carry no interval. Each is an average over a few independent 180-day windows - far fewer than the evaluation attempts - so the ordering of two plans within a few hundred dollars means nothing.
  • A cell is the share of funded accounts that got paid at least once when funding started that month, running for 180 days at the size shown on each card (2 micros at $50K). The figure beside each firm is the average dollars actually paid to you, after the profit split.
  • Most funded accounts end in a breach in this model - partly the strategy, partly our withdrawal policy of taking the maximum as soon as it is allowed. A firm's real breach rate under a more careful policy would be lower.
  • Getting paid is about the rules, not the split. Plans that let you withdraw early and often beat plans with a higher split but a large buffer to clear first.
  • Consistency rules delay payouts, they do not fail the account. A big day must be diluted by more trading before the money is released - and this strategy earns in a few big days.
  • Assumed rules are flagged. Where a firm does not publish its payout caps, the model uses a sensible cap and the card says so.
